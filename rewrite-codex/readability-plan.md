# Readability plan: first draft, review, and revised specification

## First draft (before reading or rewriting the mathematics)

A more readable mathematical paper lets a reader answer three questions at every stage: What are we trying to prove now? Why should this be the right intermediate statement? Which earlier facts justify the next inference? Improving readability therefore means more than replacing difficult sentences with shorter ones. It means exposing the structure of the argument without weakening its content. The rewritten paper should retain precise hypotheses, quantify all choices, and distinguish a proved assertion from a motivating picture or a conjectural extension.

The opening should define the objects and the desired conclusion using a small example. It should say what makes the problem difficult, what structural transformation is the main invention, and what obstruction that transformation is designed to remove. A reader should see the destination before being asked to follow technical details. A proof roadmap should name the intermediate results and explain how they fit together. Where the argument changes viewpoint—from a graph to a matrix, a weighted object, or a minimal obstruction—the rewrite should explain what information is preserved and what new freedom the viewpoint supplies.

Definitions should be local enough that readers do not have to search backward, but stable enough that the same concept does not acquire several names. Notation should have one meaning, with direction conventions made explicit. Every neighborhood convention must specify whether walks of length two count, whether the starting vertex is excluded, and whether first neighbors are removed. Small examples should expose the consequences of these distinctions. Degenerate cases, including isolated vertices, empty sets, zero weights, and equality cases, deserve explicit treatment where the proof depends on them.

Each substantial proof should begin with an informal account of its mechanism. The formal proof should then separate construction, invariant, termination, and extraction of the conclusion where those stages occur. An inequality should come with an explanation of what is being counted or charged. A contradiction argument should identify exactly which hypothesis is contradicted. If a result depends on strict positivity, finiteness, a minimality choice, or disjointness, the dependency should be visible at the point of use.

Figures should do mathematical work. Useful figures include a neighborhood example, a dependency diagram, a before-and-after transformation, an obstruction configuration, and a picture explaining a cancellation or charging argument. Color should be redundant with labels, line styles, or shapes. A schematic must say which edges are required, forbidden, or unspecified. A drawing is not proof of an assertion about all instances. Captions should explain what inference the figure supports, rather than merely repeat its title. Labels must remain legible at normal PDF scale and avoid overlapping vertices, edges, or other labels.

The rewrite should be navigable on two levels. A first reading should follow statements, roadmaps, figures, and proof summaries. A close reading should find every inference and every hypothesis needed to verify the argument. Background facts should either be proved or clearly identified as inputs. The presentation should follow the same conceptual spine while omitting details that cannot be explained in twenty minutes. Speaker notes can retain the technical qualifications and transitions that do not belong on slides.

Correctness requires an audit independent of exposition. I will track the source statements against the rewrite, check the direction of all implications and inequalities, test definitions on small examples, and explicitly record any unresolved gap. Compilation verifies syntax and layout, not mathematics. Lean can add a second kind of checking, but its scope must be stated honestly: formalizing a numerical lemma or a conditional reduction does not certify the whole graph theorem.

## Review of the first draft

The draft identifies the right goals, but it is too qualitative to guide verification. It needs concrete deliverables, explicit scope boundaries, and a record of revisions. It also risks assuming that the supplied paper is correct or that its principal theorem is already established. The rewrite must first determine what the source actually proves and must not silently repair a gap. The plan should separate fidelity to the source from endorsement of the source's mathematics. Finally, a twenty-minute talk needs a timed allocation and repeated consistency checks, not just fewer pages of the article.

## Revised specification (adopted before starting the rewrite)

1. Read all source sections without modifying them. Build a statement/dependency inventory and record any issue with exact mathematical content, not just stylistic criticism. Identify the source's principal theorem, its hypotheses, and any conjectural or conditional steps before writing an affirmative abstract.
2. Produce a self-contained LaTeX exposition in this directory. Begin with conventions and a concrete example, state the main result with its status, and include a roadmap. Explain the purpose of each construction before the details. Preserve the distinction between second walks and second neighborhoods, and between nonnegative and positive weights.
3. Add several original vector figures using TikZ. Use explicit coordinates and generous spacing. Include explanatory captions and identify schematic edges. Compile the document and inspect rendered pages, concentrating on figures, long equations, page breaks, and warning messages.
4. Verify claims by reconstructing the argument, checking elementary instances computationally when useful, and keeping a correctness ledger. If the source has a gap, explain it precisely and change the deliverable to a faithful, clearly qualified account rather than present an unsupported theorem as established.
5. Create a Beamer presentation with a total target duration of twenty minutes. Allocate time to the problem, the main mechanism, the technical heart, and the conclusion. Include notes, then revise once for mathematical consistency and once for narrative flow and visual density. Record the revisions and inspect the rendered slides.
6. After the exposition is written, formalize as much of the actual argument as feasible in Lean. Prefer meaningful definitions and substantive checked implications over vacuous restatements. State the exact trusted assumptions. Avoid `sorry` and undeclared axioms in completed proofs; report unfinished scope plainly. Compile with an available Lean toolchain and retain reproducible commands.
7. Keep a README with artifact paths, build commands, verification results, and limitations. Read other agents' work only after completing the independent deliverables; any review notes belong here. All project writes stay inside `rewrite-codex`.

Success means a reader can explain the proof's proposed mechanism, find its mathematical dependencies, distinguish verified conclusions from open obligations, and reproduce the compiled artifacts. Extra prose and extra figures are useful only insofar as they serve those goals.
